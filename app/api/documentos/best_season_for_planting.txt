The Rhythm of the Rains: A Comprehensive Guide to Nigeria's Planting Seasons for Over 100 Crops
Abstract

The planting season in Nigeria is not a singular event but a complex, regionally varied rhythm dictated by the movement of the Intertropical Convergence Zone, which creates a distinct wet-dry cycle across the country's diverse agro-ecological zones. This dissertation provides a comprehensive guide to Nigeria's planting seasons, structured by the major agricultural periods and tailored to the six geopolitical zones. Drawing on official crop calendars, meteorological forecasts, and expert agricultural advisories, the analysis identifies the optimal planting windows for over 100 crops, ranging from staple grains and root tubers to vegetables, legumes, fruits, cash crops, and spices. The dissertation argues that the key to agricultural success in Nigeria lies not in a single planting date but in the strategic alignment of crop choice, local climate patterns, and the specific risks of the season—particularly as climate change introduces greater unpredictability, including false rainfall onset and mid-season dry spells.
Part I: The Framework of Nigerian Planting Seasons
Chapter 1: Understanding Nigeria's Climatic Rhythm

Nigeria's agricultural calendar is fundamentally governed by the movement of rainfall. The country experiences a gradient from the humid south to the semi-arid north, creating a staggered and regionalized planting schedule.

Rainfall Patterns: The season is concentrated from June to September: the south receives 50-60% of its annual rainfall, while the north receives up to 90% of its rainfall in this window. This results in a clear distinction between the Main Rainy Season, the Dry Season, and transitional periods.

Nigeria's six geopolitical zones experience this rhythm differently:

    Northern Nigeria experiences a dry season of 5-7 months, making farming heavily reliant on the limited June-September rains.

    The Middle Belt acts as a transitional zone with a distinct wet and dry season.

    The South has a longer, more consistent rainy season, sometimes allowing for two cropping cycles per year.

Chapter 2: The Key Planting Windows

Broadly, Nigeria's planting calendar is divided into three strategic windows:

    The Main Season (March - July): This is the primary planting period for most staple crops. Timing varies from February/March in the south to June/July in the north.

    The Second/Early Dry Season (September - December): As the rains recede, this window allows for short-cycle crops, vegetables, and dry-season preparation, especially where residual moisture or irrigation is available.

    The Dry Season (January - March): This period is primarily for harvesting and land preparation, with planting often limited to irrigated plots.

Part II: The Seasonal Planting Guide: Over 100 Crops

Chapter 3: The Main Season (February - August) — The Core of the Food System

This is the principal planting window for Nigeria's major staples, driven by the onset and establishment of the rains.

Southern Nigeria (South West, South East, South South)
The season begins early here. Farmers in the south can begin planting as early as February/March, as the rains are more consistent.

    Staples & Cereals:

        Maize (Main): The optimal window begins in February/March, allowing for a main harvest. Also, a second crop can sometimes be planted later in the season (August/September) in areas with sufficient rainfall.

        Yam: Planting typically occurs in February/March, with harvest beginning in July/August.

        Cassava: Planting can be done in April with the established rains, though it can also be planted in October.

        Rice (Rainfed/Upland): Planting occurs in March/April.

    Vegetables:

        Tomato, Pepper, Okra: April is a peak month for planting these vegetables, capitalizing on the established rainfall and warm temperatures.

        Leafy Greens (Amaranth, Ugu): These can be planted throughout the rainy season for continuous harvest.

Northern Nigeria (North West, North East, North Central)
For the north, the window is much tighter and begins later. The start of the season is heavily dependent on the arrival of the rains, which can be variable.

    Cereals & Staples:

        Millet: Planting typically begins as the rains establish in June/July.

        Sorghum: Also planted in June/July, using a drought-resistant variety known as "guinea corn".

        Maize (Main, North): Planted in June/July when the rainy season is fully established.

    Legumes:

        Cowpea (Beans): Planting windows are very specific. The African Agricultural Technology Foundation (AATF) advises farmers in the Sahel to plant from the third week of June to early July. In the Sudan Savannah, planting should be from the first to second week of July, and in the Northern Guinea Savannah, from the third week of July to the first week of August.

Chapter 4: The Transitional Season (September - November) — The "Second Season" and Strategic Planting

As the main rains taper off, this period offers opportunities for short-cycle crops and preparation for the dry season.

The "Second Season" (South and Middle Belt):
This is a crucial window for maize in the south, often called the "second" maize crop, planted from August to September for a harvest in November/December.

October Planting (Across all zones):
October represents a transitional month: the rainy season is ending in the north, while the south still has moisture.

    North Central (Kogi, Benue, FCT): Focus on late vegetables like tomato, pepper, okra, spinach, and cowpea.

    North West (Kano, Kaduna, Katsina): The key crop here is wheat, for which the planting season typically begins in October/November under irrigation.

    South West (Oyo, Ogun): This is a good time for cassava, cocoyam, and a final push for vegetables like ugu (fluted pumpkin) and cucumber.

    South East and South South: As rainfall remains present, farmers can plant short-cycle vegetables like okra, pepper, and set up plantain suckers.

Chapter 5: Dry Season and Strategic Planning (December - March)

Planting generally slows during the driest months of December to March. However, farmers use this time for land preparation (clearing, ridging, mounding) and for dry-season farming where irrigation is available.

The Climate Risk Warning (2026 Outlook):
The 2026 weather outlook highlights a crucial shift: false starts. Farmers are warned against planting immediately after the first rains. In the south (Lagos, Ogun, Rivers), early rains may be followed by dry spells, risking seed loss. They should wait until rainfall is consistent and enough moisture is in the soil to support germination.

Conclusion: The Art of Timely Planting

The best season for planting in Nigeria is not a single month but a strategic, region-specific window. The "best" season is the one that aligns a crop's specific needs with the local climate. The 2026 predictions highlight that farmers must move beyond a fixed calendar. They must now manage climate risks like false starts and dry spells by using short-maturing varieties, water conservation techniques (mulching, tied ridges), and, where possible, irrigation. Ultimately, mastering Nigeria's seasons means adapting to its changing climate while leveraging the knowledge of its agro-ecological diversity.
