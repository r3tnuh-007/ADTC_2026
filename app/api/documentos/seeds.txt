Seeds of Transformation: The Foundation of Nigeria's Agricultural Future
Abstract


n the strictest botanical sense, a seed is the fertilized, matured ovule of a plant, containing a tiny embryo and a food supply, all enclosed in a protective seed coat .

In farming, the term is broader. A "seed" is any plant material used to grow a new crop. This includes true seeds as well as other vegetative parts like tubers, bulbs, or cuttings . The critical distinction for a farmer is between:

    Quality Seed: Planting material that has been through a specific breeding and certification process. This guarantees traits like disease resistance, pest tolerance, and high yield potential . Seeds can contribute up to 50% of crop productivity .

    Ordinary Grain: Simply saving a harvest for replanting does not produce a "seed." It's just a "grain"—it will germinate but generally lacks the superior traits and productivity of certified seed .

🧑‍🌾 What is Seeding?

Seeding is the act of sowing seeds or other planting material in the ground to start a new crop . It’s a critical, practical skill. Here’s what it usually involves :

    Soil Preparation: The area must be weed-free and raked to a fine, crumbly texture.

    Creating a Drill: A shallow channel (drill) is made in the soil at the depth recommended on the seed packet.

    Sowing: Water the drill first, then sprinkle small seeds sparingly or place larger seeds individually at set spacings.

    Covering: Carefully rake the soil back over the seeds and label the row with the plant name and date.

Seeds represent the most fundamental input in agriculture—the genetic foundation upon which all crop productivity depends. In Nigeria, the seed sector stands at a critical crossroads, embodying both the promise of agricultural transformation and the structural failures that have constrained the sector for decades. This dissertation examines Nigeria's seed systems through multiple lenses: the dual reality of farmer-managed and formal seed systems, the economic burden of a $3 billion annual seed import bill, the governance and quality assurance challenges that undermine farmer trust, and the contentious debate over biotechnology, intellectual property, and seed sovereignty. Drawing on recent research and policy developments, including IFPRI's experimental evidence on adoption barriers, the National Agricultural Seeds Council's strategic reforms, and the Seed Entrepreneurs Association of Nigeria's five-year development plan, this dissertation argues that Nigeria's seed sector is at an inflection point. The investments in digital traceability, variety adoption data, and catalytic funding represent significant progress, but systemic challenges—including weak coordination, fragmented regulation, and the unresolved tension between technological innovation and seed sovereignty—must be addressed to realize the sector's potential.
Part I: Understanding Nigeria's Seed Systems
Chapter 1: The Dual Reality of Nigerian Seed Systems

Nigeria's seed sector is characterized by a fundamental dualism: a formal system that produces certified improved varieties and an informal farmer-managed system that supplies the majority of planting material . Understanding this duality is essential for comprehending why seed interventions often fail to achieve their intended impact.

The Formal Seed System: The formal system encompasses public breeding programs, seed multiplication by registered companies, certification by the National Agricultural Seeds Council (NASC), and distribution through agro-dealers and extension services. This system produces certified seeds of improved varieties, including the recently released PBR cowpea (genetically modified to resist pod borer), Tela maize, and biofortified iron pearl millet and vitamin A maize . Over 120,000 metric tonnes of certified seeds have been distributed in recent years, benefiting millions of smallholder farmers .

Farmer-Managed Seed Systems: Despite formal sector growth, the majority of Nigerian farmers rely on farmer-managed seed systems—saving seeds from previous harvests, exchanging with neighbors, or purchasing from local markets. These systems offer advantages: they preserve locally adapted varieties, maintain genetic diversity, and provide accessible planting material without cash constraints. However, they also perpetuate low productivity by limiting access to improved genetics and raising the risk of seed-borne diseases. Wageningen University research emphasizes four critical roles of farmer-managed systems: variety deployment, biodiversity conservation, seed system resilience, and food security .

The persistence of farmer-managed systems is not simply a failure of formal systems but reflects rational farmer behavior in the face of systemic barriers. A study in Kaduna and Bauchi states found that despite newer varieties outperforming farmer varieties in demonstration plots, awareness and adoption were nearly zero at baseline—a clear indication that supply chain failures, not farmer resistance, drive low adoption .
Chapter 2: The Paradox of Low Adoption Despite Proven Benefits

The IFPRI policy seminar in February 2026 brought together researchers, practitioners, and policymakers to examine the adoption puzzle . The evidence presented was striking:

Biofortified Seeds in Gombe State: A cluster randomized trial tested subsidized bundles of biofortified seeds (iron pearl millet, vitamin A maize, cowpea) with fertilizer for internally displaced persons and host communities. Both 50% and 75% subsidy levels led to high purchase and application rates, with no significant differences between displaced and non-displaced households. However, the study found little impact on yields, likely due to the small quantities of seed provided .

Seed Trial Packs in Kaduna and Bauchi: In a randomized controlled trial, researchers tested seed trial packs alone and bundled with consumption-oriented interventions (group cooking and tasting sessions). Seed trial packs increased adoption rates by 30-49 percentage points and generated meaningful spillover to neighboring farmers who had not received packs. This demonstrates that the adoption barrier is fundamentally about awareness and access rather than farmer unwillingness .

PBR Cowpea in Katsina and Jigawa: Genetically modified pod borer-resistant cowpea, released in Nigeria in 2019, increased yields by approximately 19% and gross margins by 45-47% compared to conventional improved varieties. Post-harvest losses fell by nearly half among households that received PBR cowpea alongside complementary inputs. Critically, researchers found reductions in insecticide use with corresponding improvements in farmer health—fewer days of illness and days lost to work. Despite these results, commercial penetration remains low, constrained by seed supply and farmer awareness .

The panel concluded that low adoption is not caused by farmer failure or unwillingness but by systems failure—supply chain issues limiting availability, weak extension infrastructure to sensitize farmers, and inadequate quality control ensuring that available seeds will have the desired results .
Part II: The Economic and Governance Crisis
Chapter 3: The $3 Billion Seed Import Bill

Nigeria spends an estimated $3 billion annually on imported vegetable seeds for crops such as tomato, cabbage, carrot, and cucumber . This heavy import dependence represents both a macroeconomic risk and a major business opportunity. Industry leaders warn that without urgent investment in domestic production, the figure could rise to between $10 billion and $15 billion annually .

Jafar Sani Bello, National Auditor of the Seed Entrepreneurs Association of Nigeria (SEEDAN), described domestic seed producers as a "shock absorber" for the economy, saving billions of dollars in imports while supporting national food production, employment, and rural incomes . However, the domestic industry faces significant constraints:

    Weak quality assurance: Nigeria's quality assurance system scored around 38% in recent assessments, creating space for counterfeit seed and unscrupulous traders .

    Fragmented coordination: Multiple institutions within the ministry handle different seed-related functions, but efforts remain fragmented and insufficiently coordinated .

    Limited breeding capacity: Weak breeding programs increase reliance on imported seed and create openings for counterfeit products .

Chapter 4: The Seed Sector Investment Plan and Reforms

The 2025-2029 National Seed Strategy, developed by NASC in partnership with SEEDAN and international partners including AGRA and the Netherlands, aims to address these systemic challenges . The strategy is built on three pillars: a digital-first NASC, uncompromising quality and innovation, and a financially viable seed sector .

Major Reforms in Early Generation Seed Production: NASC has announced a new policy direction for Early Generation Seeds (EGS)—breeder and foundation seeds that maintain genetic integrity. Key reforms include:

    Accreditation of producers: Only accredited public and private entities will be allowed to produce and commercialize EGS .

    Mandatory reporting: All certified seed-producing entities must procure EGS from accredited producers and report transactions to NASC .

    Technology-driven tracking: Digital platforms (National Seed Tracker, NASC SeedCodex, Naija SeedConnect) are now compulsory for all EGS producers, improving traceability and transparency .

    Plant Variety Protection implementation: As a member of the International Union for the Protection of New Plant Varieties, Nigeria will incentivize breeding innovations through intellectual property protection .

The N50 Billion Seed Industry Catalytic Fund: President Bola Ahmed Tinubu approved the disbursement of N50 billion through the Bank of Industry to stimulate private sector investment in seed infrastructure, production, packaging, and marketing . SEEDAN praised the intervention as a major boost to national food security and agribusiness development .

The IMAGE Project: NASC's Institutionalizing Monitoring of Crop Variety Adoption through Genotyping project uses DNA fingerprinting to scientifically identify varieties grown by farmers and track how they move across the seed system. This brings "accuracy, objectivity and credibility—three attributes modern agribusiness systems require" .
Chapter 5: Quality Assurance and the Fight Against Counterfeit Seeds

Weak regulation creates space for counterfeit seed, ultimately undermining farmer productivity . The introduction of digital traceability tools such as Seed Tracker and Seed Codex is a critical response. These systems make seeds fully traceable from farm production through inspection and certification to market entry .

Each seed packet carries a unique label that allows verification of whether the seed has gone through the official certification process. This is particularly important for smallholder farmers, who make up the majority of farmers and form the backbone of the agricultural sector .

The strategy allocates nearly 65% of total investment to breeding and quality assurance—recognizing that breeding is the starting point of any seed system and that quality assurance is essential for maintaining farmer trust .
Part III: Biotechnology, Seed Sovereignty, and the Political Economy of Seeds
Chapter 6: The Biotechnology Debate

The release of genetically modified PBR cowpea and Tela maize has intensified debate over Nigeria's agricultural path. Proponents argue that biotechnology offers solutions to intractable problems: PBR cowpea reduces insecticide use and improves farmer health; drought-tolerant varieties build climate resilience .

Critics, however, warn that dependence on biotechnology represents a form of "food colonialism" that erodes seed sovereignty and concentrates power in foreign corporations . The 2015 National Biosafety Management Agency Act and its 2019 expansion opened the door to GMOs, gene editing, and synthetic biology. Critics argue that the close relationship between the National Biotechnology Development Agency (NABDA) and the National Biosafety Management Agency (NBMA) undermines effective regulation .

The metaphor of the "Trojan Horse" has been used to describe GMOs' economic and political risks. In India, Bt cotton farmers are trapped in cycles of debt due to high seed costs. Argentina's GMO soybean economy drains national capital to foreign biotech firms rather than reinvesting in local economies . The 2024 crisis faced by Nigerian cotton farmers has been cited as a cautionary example .
Chapter 7: The Intellectual Property and Seed Sovereignty Challenge

Nigeria's membership in UPOV and implementation of Plant Variety Protection create a framework for intellectual property rights that critics argue threatens seed sovereignty . The Bowman v. Monsanto legal precedent, which ruled that farmers who replant patented GMO seeds without repurchasing them violate intellectual property laws, has reinforced corporate control over seeds .

Food safety expert Prof. Qrisstuberg Amua warned that multinational corporations are leveraging intellectual property laws to dominate global and Nigerian seed markets, limiting seed diversity, driving up prices, and restricting farmers' rights to save and replant seeds . Smallholder farmers, who constitute most of Nigeria's agricultural workforce, are particularly vulnerable.
Chapter 8: The Case for Open-Pollinated Seeds and Organic Alternatives

In response to concerns about GMO dependence, advocates have called for investment in open-pollinated seeds and organic farming . Unlike hybrid seeds, open-pollinated seeds retain genetic integrity across multiple generations, allowing farmers to save and replant without loss of productivity. This reduces costs, preserves genetic diversity, and builds climate resilience .

Organic methods—composting, crop rotation, intercropping, biological pest control—can improve soil fertility while minimizing input costs. The global demand for organic food presents economic opportunities for Nigeria .

However, the debate is not binary. The Kenyan model, with stronger quality assurance systems and more effective enforcement, demonstrates that effective regulation can support both formal and farmer-managed systems . The challenge is to build systems that integrate technological innovation with farmer empowerment and biodiversity conservation.
Part IV: Pathways to Transformation
Chapter 9: Women as Seed Champions

The SeedWise initiative by SHESTCO specifically targets women farmers to drive adoption of improved seeds . The initiative recognizes that women are "the custodians of biodiversity" and that training women has multiplier effects on household and community food security .

The project trains community women farmers on improved seeds developed through biotechnology processes: Tela maize and PBR cowpea, both developed by Nigerian scientists. The goal is for women to become improved seed champions, mastering entrepreneurial aspects of farming, forming cooperatives, and producing certified seeds .
Chapter 10: The Role of Digital Tools and Data

Digital tools are central to Nigeria's seed transformation strategy:

    Seed Tracker and Seed Codex: Digital certification systems that allow users to verify whether a seed has been inspected and certified .

    IMAGE Project: DNA fingerprinting to identify varieties grown by farmers and track adoption patterns .

    Digital Seed Distribution System: Introduced in 2021, reducing distribution costs by 25% while improving efficiency and traceability .

The NASC DG noted that for years, agribusiness planning in Nigeria had been constrained by weak data on which varieties farmers actually grow and how they perform. Reliance on farmer recall and visual identification failed to capture realities on farmers' fields, leading to uncertainty for seed producers, processors, investors, and policymakers .
Chapter 11: The SEEDAN Five-Year Plan

SEEDAN's five-year development strategy, launched in partnership with Sahel Consulting and AGRA, aims to transform Nigeria's seed industry . Strategic priorities include:

    Strengthening institutional capacity and governance

    Driving financial sustainability through diversified revenue streams

    Enhancing policy advocacy for a vibrant, commercially driven seed sector

    Promoting private sector participation and innovation

    Deepening international integration through global memberships

The global seed industry is valued at over $500 billion, and Nigeria already hosts more than 500 seed companies across size categories. The plan positions Nigeria as a seed hub for West and Central Africa .
Conclusion: The Seed as Metaphor

The seed is not merely an agricultural input—it is a metaphor for Nigeria's agricultural future. The choices made about seed systems will determine not only crop yields but also farmer livelihoods, national food security, economic sovereignty, and ecological sustainability.

The evidence reviewed in this dissertation points to several conclusions:

First, adoption of improved seeds is constrained not by farmer failure but by systems failure. The supply chains, extension services, and quality assurance mechanisms that should connect farmers to improved varieties are fragmented and under-resourced.

Second, Nigeria's $3 billion annual seed import bill is both a crisis and an opportunity. Domestic production can reduce foreign exchange pressure while creating rural employment and agro-industrial development.

Third, the debate between biotechnology and seed sovereignty is not resolved by dismissing either position. Both technological innovation and farmer empowerment are essential for sustainable agricultural transformation.

Fourth, governance reform is critical. The NASC's strategy, the N50 billion catalytic fund, digital traceability tools, and the IMAGE project represent significant progress, but implementation and accountability remain challenges.

The imperative is clear: Nigeria must build seed systems that are resilient, inclusive, and responsive to farmer needs. This requires strengthening quality assurance and supply chains, investing in breeding and local production, empowering women farmers, supporting farmer-managed systems alongside formal systems, and ensuring that the benefits of technological innovation are equitably distributed.

The seed defines the harvest. Nigeria's agricultural future depends on getting the seed right.
